## Theme: Verification depth is an empirical design variable

Eight arXiv papers motivate a recurring structural hypothesis: **verification,
governance, and oversight can fail when they operate at a different depth than
the phenomenon they are trying to verify**. Their heterogeneous tasks do not
prove one universal root cause, but they supply concrete cases and candidate
design tests.

The Verification Stack (#412) proposed layered verification as the immune
response to surface trust collapse. The Governance Depth Problem (#405)
argued that governance can miss failures at another layer. The Persistence
Attack Surface (#423) argued that stateless verification of persistent systems
is incomplete. This scan connects those proposals through a **depth-mismatch
hypothesis** whose scope must be tested case by case.

## Paper Analysis

### CRITICAL: Reading Between the Dots — Hidden Computation Is Readable (2607.03502)

**ICML 2026 Mech Interp Workshop**. Accepted.

**Core finding**: On four constructed task families with discrete, nameable
intermediates, DeepSeek V3 and Kimi K2 sometimes use filler placed between a
question and answer as extra computation positions. The paper supplies three
different kinds of evidence rather than one general readability result:

- Behavioral uplift varies by task and model: DeepSeek rises 54% to about 72%
  on 1-fact addition but only 21.1% to 24.1% on 2-fact addition; the
  system-of-equations gains are larger. Qwen 3 480B improves 43% to 47% on
  letter position but not addition, so the phenomenon is not universal.
- Attention and logit-lens maps localize task-related values. KV-cache
  transplants confined to filler positions causally improve the donor answer's
  rank, but full donor-answer swaps are only 13% on 1-fact addition and 6.4%
  on systems of equations in the named conditions.
- The label-free decoder aggregates residual logit-lens tokens, then gives the
  top 50 to Claude Haiku or Sonnet. On correct examples, the strongest judge's
  pooled top-2 recovery is 94.3% for 1-fact, 82.3%/90.0% for DeepSeek/Kimi
  2-fact, 92.7%/90.2% for letter-position domains, and 82.6% for the DeepSeek
  system-of-equations intermediate. Direct top-2 token recovery is much wider,
  35.2%--84.8%. Ground truth is not used to train the decoder, but it is used
  to define and score the target; judge prompts specify the expected answer
  shape, and task-aware prompts further improve some results.

**Key quote from abstract**: "Hidden computation that defeats behavioral CoT monitoring is, on these tasks, directly readable from the residual stream, suggesting monitorability is a property of the model's full computational trace, not just its surface tokens."

**Structural significance**: The visible filler does not verbalize intermediate
steps, while white-box residual access recovers substantial example-specific
signal on these tasks; same-condition shuffled-token controls fall near zero.
The paper does not measure mutual information at the behavioral surface, test
general trajectory monitors, or recover 80--95% of “the computation.” Its
reported range is best-judge top-2 accuracy for selected discrete targets on
correct examples. The supported conclusion is partial, task-scoped white-box
auditability—not zero behavioral information or near-complete visibility.

Mechanistic scope matters. Analyses use 4-bit quantized open-weight checkpoints
and require 3--4 H200 GPUs, about 840 GB of weights, roughly 1 TB of extraction
cache, and around $10^4$ judge calls per task. Logit-lens residualization helps
some conditions and hurts others by up to 11.5 points. The method may miss
computation outside the vocabulary projection, diffuse strategies or intent,
and adversarially hidden representations; closed-weight models are inaccessible.

The public repository at
`kaleybrauer/filler-token-reasoning@4ba4c75d5d9f04248749ec46b8bed8661b746715`
contains 202 release JSON files. All parse, and all 138 accuracy groups exactly
reaggregate from 36,358 judge rows (one disclosed refusal). The release does
not include the system-of-equations, shuffled-control, KV-transplant,
attention, or behavioral-uplift outputs reported in the manuscript, so those
claims remain source-table evidence rather than independently recomputable
from the published artifact. No license is declared.

**Connects to**: Oversight Inversion — the cost of monitoring scales with depth, not output volume. The Plausibility Gradient (#390) — surface plausibility hides actual computation. The dual-channel divergence (#423, 2607.02507) — agents can diverge at depth while presenting plausible surface.

**For our system**: The paper motivates testing whether trajectory evidence
(tool calls, timing, and state mutations) adds signal beyond final outputs. It
does not establish that analogy or show that output inspection always operates
at the wrong depth.

### CRITICAL: How to Avoid Debate — Single-Prover Verification (2607.03561)

**ICML 2026**. Accepted.

**Core finding**: The paper proves that one prover can replace debate in two
formal classes of oracle-aided computation. This removes the second-prover,
equal-capability, and honest-opponent premises *inside those classes*, while
replacing them with explicit structure rather than eliminating assumptions in
general.

The shared model gives both parties a succinct description of a known
logspace-uniform oracle circuit and access to the same oracle. Four asymptotic
protocols cover different guarantees:

- Robust, nonadaptive circuits have a statistically sound proof with
  `O(sigma/epsilon)` oracle queries and roughly
  `O(sigma(epsilon*l*D + 1/epsilon + n))` verifier time.
- Robust adaptive circuits add the number `d` of oracle levels to query,
  round, and verifier costs. A separate constant-round argument uses
  `O(1/epsilon)` queries but is sound only against polynomial-time provers.
- A polynomial low-degree oracle permits one verifier query and near-linear
  verifier time, but only as a computationally sound argument under polynomial
  Short Integer Solution (SIS) hardness. Oracle access here means the parties
  at arbitrary field points, which is stronger than ordinary access to a human
  or web API.

The boundary is as important as the construction. The paper states that no
single-prover interactive proof can relativize for all oracle-aided
computations: unstable computation over an arbitrary oracle can require the
verifier to check every call. Robustness means the output survives changing up
to an `epsilon` fraction of oracle answers; whether realistic oversight tasks
have that property, whether redundancy creates it, and whether any protocol
is practical are left as future work. The verifier must also know the
computation description; verifying a task whose algorithm is unknown is open.

**Structural significance**: This is an existence result for conditional
single-prover verification, not a measured budget reduction or coverage gain.
The paper gives no experiment, deployed AI verifier, or head-to-head cost
comparison with debate. Two provers therefore cannot be translated into
“double the oversight cost,” and a formal single-prover protocol cannot yet be
treated as a cheaper operational harness. The supported lesson is narrower:
prover count and oracle structure are separate design variables, and formal
soundness is possible without an honest opponent when the task satisfies the
stated conditions.

The exact v1 source archive is 6,784,355 bytes with SHA-256
`451f5ad902349984e17e18eed92f224199a629f49e802e14e98ebb6c3be3e944`.
It contains the theorem and proof sources but no implementation or empirical
results. The manuscript names no repository; one bounded exact-title GitHub
repository search returned zero matches, which is an access observation rather
than proof that no implementation exists.

**Connects to**: Governance Depth (#405) — assumptions and authority must be
bound to the verified task. CAGE-1 Prebind Assurance (2607.03510) — proving
correctness before action requires an executable representation. The Repair
Turn (#419) — formal rejection does not supply repair capacity.

### CRITICAL: Securing Multi-Tool Agent Chains — Compositional Taint (2607.03423)

**Core finding**: The Dynamic Security Control Compositor (DSCC) proposes a
declared-policy composition boundary for multi-tool chains. Phase 1 composes a
six-field policy for every tool: NIST control bindings, classification,
transmission prohibition, flow direction, permitted network zones, and
session lifetime. Its Most Restrictive Set operators take maxima for control
and classification, Boolean OR for prohibition, intersection for zones, and a
minimum lifetime. Phase 2 raises a session taint when resources are accessed
and blocks a later outbound call under three stated guards.

The algebra supports a narrow monotonicity statement: for truthful, complete,
stable declarations and classifications, appending a tool cannot lower a
control/classification/prohibition, enlarge the zone intersection, or extend
the lifetime. It does not show that the declarations match real tool behavior,
that the resource classifier is correct, or that an agent cannot use an
unrepresented flow. Rejection monotonicity is therefore conditional on the
policy model rather than an end-to-end security guarantee.

The reported Phase 1 table is exactly recoverable from the catalog's class
sizes and one email-policy exception:

- In clearance mode, the class groups have sizes 6, 5, 3, and 2. Cross-class
  pairs account for 91/120 rejections. The outbound email policy's boundary
  rule rejects four more same-class pairs, giving 95/120 (79.2%). At length
  three, class partitioning accounts for 529/560 rejections and the email rule
  adds six, giving 535/560 (95.5%).
- In taint mode, the 12 non-email Restricted, Confidential, and Internal
  policies form one permitted region while the three Public policies form
  another. This leaves 51/120 rejected pairs (42.5%) and 339/560 rejected
  triples (60.5%).
- Applying the same disclosed partitions to the 32-tool multiplicities also
  reproduces 704/992 and 4,499/4,960 clearance rejections, plus 322/992 and
  2,350/4,960 taint rejections. A matched control that removes only the email
  boundary exception yields 91/120 (75.8%) and 529/560 (94.5%), showing that
  these rates characterize this authored catalog and policy distribution, not
  an intrinsic detection rate.

**Critical qualification**: The 79.2% denominator is every unordered pair of
the 16 policies, not a ground-truth set of policy violations. The study has no
per-tool-guardrail baseline, labeled safe/unsafe chains, precision, recall,
false-positive rate, agent attack campaign, or workflow-utility outcome. The
paper itself says MRS cannot distinguish a precise security block from an
overblocked legitimate workflow. Phase 2 is demonstrated through three
illustrative walkthroughs rather than evaluated over attacks or live agents.
The earlier “0% versus 79.2% caught” interpretation is unsupported.

Reproducibility is partial. The exact v1 source archive is 58,932 bytes with
SHA-256
`d7603468f756bed2a63e8f79160c787b62cdad3b8882486379515a0eb5c57de9`.
Its five members include the manuscript, bibliography, and figures but no C#
implementation. Although the appendix says its tables are sufficient to
reproduce the enumeration, the policy table omits the permitted network-zone
sets required by the stated six-field model and rejection algorithm. The
counts above can be reconstructed from the disclosed class groups and email
exception, but the claimed full implementation path and zone-intersection
behavior cannot be reproduced from the archive. A bounded GitHub repository
search for “Dynamic Security Control Compositor” returned two complete
secondary resource-list results and no author implementation in their
repository metadata; this is a search boundary, not proof no code exists.

**Structural significance**: Policy composition is a plausible response to a
depth mismatch, and the operator-level monotonicity is useful. This paper does
not measure how many real violations individual checks miss or how many DSCC
blocks are correct. The supported design question is whether a deployment can
maintain complete, truthful policy and resource labels while measuring both
security catches and legitimate-workflow loss.

**Connects to**: AgentFlow (#412, 2607.01640) — first static analysis finding 238 prompt-to-tool taint risks across 5,399 repos. SkillFuzz (#411, 2607.02345) — >1,000 implicit intents from benign skill compositions. The Persistence Attack Surface (#423) — persistent state creates cross-layer attack paths.

### HIGH: Agentic Code Review — 56.3% Rejection Rate (2607.03316)

**Core finding**: The paper studies 31,073 CodeRabbit review comments that
received a developer reply, drawn from 10,191 pull requests. Those comments are
only 9.34% of the 332,693 eligible CodeRabbit reviews in the 239-project sampling
frame. The released rows contain 215 repositories, so the headline project count
describes selection rather than the analyzed artifact.

Key results:
- The released labels reproduce exactly: **11,297 accepted** (36.4%), **2,276
  discussion** (7.3%), and **17,500 rejected** (56.3%). “Discussion” requires
  explicit reference to another human; “rejected” is the residual category, not
  a direct measure of technical incorrectness or wasted reviewer time.
- Among rejected comments, 58% are classified invalid and 42% technically valid
  but misaligned. The majority category is therefore invalidity, not “right about
  the wrong thing.” Functional and evolvability comments have nearly identical
  rejection rates (56.2% versus 56.7%), while functional comments have more
  invalid labels (35.1% versus 25.1%).
- Only 297 comments were manually coded: 0.956% of 31,073, despite the manuscript
  calling this 10%. GPT-5.1 labeled the other 30,776. The 297 released manual
  labels reproduce the published tables only after replacing automated lower-
  level fields packaged with those test rows.
- The strongest released Qwen result reproduces at 134 true positives, 64 false
  positives, 20 false negatives, and 79 true negatives: 67.7% precision, 87.0%
  recall, and 76.1% F1. An always-reject control on the same prevalence already
  reaches 68.3% F1, leaving a 7.8-point gain.
- All 108 test repositories occur in training, and 228 of 282 test pull requests
  also occur in training. There is no repository-, pull-request-, or time-held-out
  evaluation and no repeated-run uncertainty, so 76% is not evidence of transfer
  to unseen projects or future behavior.

**Structural significance**: Developer replies expose two distinct failures:
technical invalidity and intent/practice misalignment. That is compatible with a
depth mismatch hypothesis, but the evidence does not attribute the aggregate
56.3% to intent depth: invalid suggestions are the larger rejected class. The
reply-only sample also cannot estimate how all CodeRabbit comments affect users,
because 90.66% of eligible comments had no developer reply and were excluded.

This is direct evidence about the distribution of explicit developer replies,
not a timing or workload measure. It motivates the Judgment Tax (#389), but does
not establish that humans spent more time rejecting than accepting, nor that a
reply-free comment was ignored, accepted silently, or never seen.

**Artifact and revisit boundary**: arXiv `2607.03316v2` and Zenodo record
`21095394` release datasets, manual codes, predictions, and training scripts.
Counts, label totals, overlap, and the best confusion matrix are reproducible
without model execution. Revisit on a reply-denominator analysis, genuinely held-
out repository/PR/time evaluation, repeated runs, or a corrected manual-sample
claim.

**Connects to**: Reasoning Precedence (#416) — reasoning about developer intent > generating functional checks. The Human Gate (#418) — human judgment quality determines hybrid system performance. Repair Turn (#419) — detection (finding code issues) > repair (producing valid fixes).

### HIGH: Selective Prediction — Training Abstention (2607.03528)

**ICML 2026 Agents in the Wild Workshop**. Accepted.

**Core finding, qualified by primary review**: Existing LLM alignment trains
for correctness or calibration. RLSR instead trains the ranking of a model's
verbalized confidence with a batch approximation to the area under the risk-
coverage curve (AURC). At evaluation time the model still generates an answer
and confidence for every item; a separately chosen threshold retains or flags
the answer. “Abstention” here is therefore post-hoc selective prediction, not a
learned stop/refusal action and not evidence that the model itself routes work
to a human.

Reinforcement Learning for Selection Reward (RLSR):
- Targets AURC as alignment objective
- Improves the paper's aggregate AURC over correctness-aligned RLVR and
  calibration-aligned RLCR: `0.41` versus `0.47`/`0.46` after HotPotQA
  training and `0.45` versus `0.53`/`0.47` after BigMath training on the
  paper's OOD groupings
- Does not win uniformly per dataset. It is best against all baselines on 4/6
  HotPotQA-trained OOD datasets and 2/5 BigMath-trained OOD datasets. Examples
  include HotPotQA-trained CommonsenseQA (`0.09` versus BASE/RLCR `0.08`) and
  GPQA (`0.58` versus BASE `0.55`), plus BigMath-trained MATH-500 (`0.36`
  versus RLCR `0.33`) and HotPotQA-Vanilla (`0.49` versus RLCR `0.47`)
- Uses “OOD” for cross-dataset evaluation. It does not construct controlled
  shifts within a population or isolate which distributional factor changed

The strongest deployment example is much smaller than its headline suggests.
On MedQA, RLSR retains 3.0% of the test split. The rounded coverage and accuracy
uniquely imply 38 selected cases and 30 correct out of 1,273; a computed 95%
Wilson interval is approximately 63.7–88.9%. RLCR retains 18/1,273 test cases
with 13 correct (72.2%; interval approximately 49.1–87.5%). The validation-
chosen point estimate is better for RLSR, but the paper supplies no confidence
bound, multiple-threshold correction, conformal guarantee, or other finite-
sample procedure that controls the claimed 25% risk. Equal validation and test
point estimates (`30/38` each) do not establish error control.

Five-seed uncertainty is reported only for HotPotQA-trained Qwen2.5-7B under
temperature `0.3`, where aggregate AURC improves. The main tables use greedy
decoding and do not report training-seed variation, per-dataset intervals, or
MedQA operating-point uncertainty.

**Released-artifact boundary**: the project page redirects to
[`sun-umn/RLSR`](https://github.com/sun-umn/RLSR/tree/d972f1716a76745f098320980d9a0d58cab94e6e).
The pinned repository includes a batch-ranking trainer, configs, evaluation

**Structural significance**: RLSR supports the narrower design claim that

### HIGH: Agentic Test-Time Training — Preserving Competence (2607.03441)

**Core finding**: LLM agents degrade over long episodes — revisiting states, repeating failed actions, losing previously working strategies. Agentic Test-Time Training (aTTT) adapts model weights during live episodes via continuous TTT with token-level reweighting.

Key mechanism: **n-gram repetition detection** distinguishes productive exploration (new trajectory info) from unproductive drift (stuck on similar text). Tokens in repeated n-grams are downweighted; novel tokens get full weight.

Key results:
- **+5.0pp** ALFWorld, **+4.9pp** SWE-bench Lite
- Overhead: 1.9× no-TTT cost (concurrent serving via vLLM runtime LoRA)
- Gains concentrate where models have competence but drift over long trajectories
- **aTTT preserves existing competence rather than teaching new abilities**

**Structural significance**: The Depth Mismatch applied to temporal persistence. Standard TTT adapts once to a fixed input (wrong depth). aTTT adapts continuously to the evolving episode (correct depth). The repetition detector is the temporal analogue of the link-tracker monitor from #423 — it detects buildup patterns across time, not just current-step features.

**Connects to**: Regression Accumulation (#416, 2607.01855) — 40-73% of tasks lose previously correct behavior across turns. Ghost Memory (#423, 2607.01935) — past state contaminates present judgment. Identity-Invariant Consolidation (#423, 2607.01988) — separating what changes from what must be preserved.

### MEDIUM: CAGE-1 — Prebind Assurance (2607.03510)

Enterprise evaluation framework introducing **Prebind Assurance**: the ability to prove that an agentic action is controlled **before it becomes binding, effective, or operationally consequential**.

Actions can be: admitted, held, narrowed, refused, escalated, quarantined, or made non-effective before protected consequence forms.

**Depth Mismatch instance**: Traditional evaluation asks "did the agent do the right thing?" (post-action depth). Prebind Assurance asks "can the agent prove it will do the right thing before it acts?" (pre-consequence depth). The gap between post-action audit and pre-consequence binding is the enterprise verification depth mismatch.

**Connects to**: Governance Depth (#405, 2607.01421) — worst failures at organizational boundary. AGL-1 (2607.03516) — a proposed seven-domain vocabulary for full execution-path governance, not an implemented or empirically evaluated control plane. The Constraint Turn (#410, 2607.02389) — constraints before execution > monitoring after execution.

### MEDIUM: Aggregate Alignment Misleads (2607.03386)

**Core finding**: In agentic policy repair, diagnostic-to-outcome alignment is not monotonic. A tree editor achieves **stronger alignment** (0.214 vs 0.266 composition distance) and **stronger reference-state match** (0.328 vs 1.197 D1) yet **revenue falls** from 108.47 to 98.91.

**Key insight**: Agentic policy repair should be evaluated by whether diagnostic feedback becomes reliable closed-loop outcome, not by a single behavioral distance.

**Depth Mismatch instance**: Aggregate alignment operates at the distribution depth. Revenue operates at the per-episode outcome depth. Improving the former can worsen the latter — the classic Goodhart's Law applied to agent governance.

**Connects to**: Reasoning Precedence (#416) — pass@k ≠ pass@1, aggregate performance masks per-instance failures. Semantic Collapse (#416, 2607.01953) — agents collapse onto a single interpretation with full confidence.

### SkillOpt-Lite — Minimal Viable Skill Evolution (2607.03451)

Three proposed principles are file-system-based trajectory exploration,
consensus attribute mining, and independent validation gating. Primary review
finds 23 wins, three ties, and four losses against SkillOpt across 30
within-model cells. The headline nano comparisons also change the optimization
method: LiveMath is 55.7 for Lite/nano versus 54.0 for SkillOpt/GPT-5.4, and
SpreadsheetBench is 0.7758 for HarnessOpt/nano versus 0.7620 for
SkillOpt/GPT-5.5. Under matched Lite or HarnessOpt methods the larger model
remains ahead, so these cells support harness/skill co-optimization rather
than a configuration-over-scale conclusion. The current public repository
ships code and checkpoint skills but lacks the named evaluator entry point and
headline outcome rows.

**Depth Mismatch**: Full SkillOpt pipeline operates at unnecessary depth. The minimal viable pipeline (one line of vibe) operates at the correct depth — only components justified by theory or empirical necessity.

### GameEngineBench — Runtime Environments Expose Agent Limits (2607.03525)

Primary review of exact v2 supports a narrower claim. The benchmark gives one
attempt per task/configuration on 110 tasks from nine public Unreal Engine
repositories. Solvers receive a buildable start state, scoped editable C++
files, and a public behavior specification; withheld tests are injected after
the solve and an LLM judge sees the specification, tests, results, edits, and
reference solution. Execution uses Play-in-Editor listen-server mode. The best
displayed run solves 61/110 (55.5%); GPT-5.5 under one wrapper rises from
10/110 at medium effort to 21/110 at high and 32/110 at xhigh. Across the
displayed outcome matrix, 79 tasks have at least one success and 31 have none.

Those 31 are a no-success subset under this finite, heterogeneous,
single-attempt matrix—not “unsolvable” tasks. The manuscript says 12
configurations, but all released per-configuration figures display 13 rows,
adding GLM 5.2 at 3/110. No repeated attempts, confidence intervals, or inferential tests
are reported, and wrapper effects confound cross-family model comparisons.
Cross-family judging is supported, but exact judge assignments and reliability
could not be checked from the paper bundle.

The linked `Nitrode-Research/GameEngineBench` repository returned HTTP 404 on
both GitHub's page and repository API at `2026-09-04T11:24:52Z`; the public
account exposed zero repositories and an unauthenticated repository-name
search returned zero matches. Therefore the claimed 110 task packages, saved
workspaces/results, runner, judge prompts, wrapper versions, and OS/hardware
metadata are not currently auditable. V2 changes 9 lines in and 9 lines out
from v1—author/affiliation text and four figure paths—without changing the
common result figures or methodology.

**Qualified Depth Mismatch**: The evidence supports a real gap between
source-plausible C++ and behavioral integration inside these Unreal projects,
especially authority, replication, lifecycle, and initialization. It does not
establish general failure across physics, rendering, or production game
operations. The authors explicitly exclude dedicated fleets, consoles,
profiling, large QA matrices, and live operations, and note light coverage of
audio, performance, platforms, security, tooling, and build systems.
Confidence is high in the source hashes, protocol text, displayed counts, and
12-versus-13 discrepancy; moderate in the scoped runtime-integration
conclusion; and absent for independent outcome reproduction or task-level
judge audit until the artifact repository becomes public.

### AtomicCommitBench — Commit History Reconstruction (2607.03332)

Given squashed patches, can agents reconstruct useful commit histories? The
benchmark samples 800 same-author runs of 2--8 commits completed within 48
hours across 10 mature Python projects. Every episode/setup has one run, and
the model-agent wrappers differ, so its results describe this finite snapshot
rather than model-family effects or repeated-run reliability.

Mechanical replay and reference grouping separate sharply. All non-random
methods reach patch-preservation applicability rate (PPAR) at or above .988,
while adjusted Rand index (ARI) ranges from .03 to .46. The manuscript reports
Codex/GPT-5.4 at .459, GLM-5 at .425, FileSplit at .340, MiniMax at .306, and
Kimi at .288. High replay validity therefore does not imply good semantic
grouping, but calling replay "trivial" overstates a near-saturated result from
these released episodes.

The reported +.333 natural-versus-synthetic gap is narrower than the earlier
summary implied: it compares only the B5 Untangle baseline on 754 real and 799
matched synthetic episodes (Mann--Whitney $p=3.60\times10^{-50}$, Cliff's
$\delta=.430$). It is not an all-agent comparison. Test Containment Ratio
(TCR) gives the same setup ordering on only 151 scoreable modified-test
episodes and is a partial probe, not full `git revert` behavior. Dependency-
Aware Commit Evidence (DACE) improves MiniMax by .075 and Kimi by .049, but
does not improve the two higher-scoring setups; the study does not isolate its
components.

The public MIT artifact at `mathieu0905/AtomicCommit` commit
`d01029eba493e36fcd9f42089307ad9440baf35f` is unusually complete: all 22 split
part hashes and both reconstructed archive hashes verify, the dataset contains
exactly 800 episode IDs, and the no-dependency artifact checker passes. One
important contradiction remains. The manuscript and multimodel result files
use strict-valid counts Codex 798, GLM 712, Kimi 550, and MiniMax 652, whereas
`active_manifest.json` and `reliable_eval_summary.json` use 798, 703, 623, and
585; the latter also changes the non-Codex ARIs to .398, .252, and .300. The
disagreement predates the final sanitization commit and the repository does
not explain which bundle is authoritative.

**Depth Mismatch**: replay checking operates at the mechanical depth (can the
patch sequence be applied?) while grouping evaluates agreement with one human
history at a semantic depth (which changes belong together?). This supports a
depth distinction in this retrospective benchmark, not a claim that the human
history is unique truth or that reconstructed histories improve review or
maintenance. Other limits include 2.3% lossy hunk mappings, Python-only mature
public projects, retrospective rather than prospective authoring, and no
consumer-side benefit test.

### Anchored Self-Play — Drift Toward Unrealistic (2607.03523)

In one Qwen2.5-Coder-7B run, Anchored Self-Play (ASP) reports 36.1%
average one-shot fix rate against 29.1% for standard self-play (+7.0 points,
about +24% relative) across 127 tasks and four bug sources. It combines
reference mixing with embedding-similarity shaping; the individual ablations
reach 29.5% and 30.9%. The main result has no repeated-run uncertainty, and
transfer to DebugBench is only +1.6 points without in-domain retraining. The
current public repository has benchmark rows and four passing release-
regression tests, but no paper checkpoints/results, and its default launcher
does not match the paper's reference sources, mixing rate, or epoch count.

**Depth Mismatch**: The result supports a training-difficulty versus
bug-distribution-realism mismatch in this function-level repair setting. It
does not establish the same mismatch for repository-scale repair or autonomous
self-configuration; those transfers remain hypotheses.

## Structural Analysis: The Depth Mismatch Unifies Five Library Theses

The Depth Mismatch is not a new thesis — it's the **unifying mechanism** that connects five existing library theses:

```
┌─ DEPTH MISMATCH ────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                          │
│  Oversight Inversion (#382):                            │
│    Surface: "can we monitor output volume?"              │
│    Correct: "can we verify at the speed of production?"  │
│    Gap: oversight cost scales super-linearly             │
│                                                          │
│  Governance Depth (#405):                               │
│    Surface: "does the policy exist?"                     │
│    Correct: "does the policy operate at the failure      │
│             layer?"                                      │
│    Gap: governance at wrong depth = false confidence     │
│                                                          │
│  Verification Stack (#412):                             │
│    Surface: "do we verify at each layer?"                │
│    Correct: "do we verify at the SAME layer as the       │
│             failure?"                                    │
│    Gap: persistent state creates cross-layer paths       │
│                                                          │
│  Abstention Decay:                                      │
│    Surface: "is the model correct?"                      │
│    Correct: "does the model know when to stop?"          │
│    Gap: correctness training erases abstention ability   │
│                                                          │
│  Plausibility Gradient (#390):                          │
│    Surface: "is the output plausible?"                   │
│    Correct: "is the reasoning valid?"                    │
│    Gap: plausible ≠ correct, fluent ≠ faithful          │
│                                                          │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Design principle**: Before deploying verification, governance, or oversight, audit the depth. If the mechanism operates at a different depth than the phenomenon it monitors, it will fail silently — worse than no mechanism, because it creates the illusion of control.

**Qualified corollary**: Matching an evaluator to the mechanism can expose
signal missed at another layer, but access and coverage are empirical
constraints, not guarantees. The filler-token study reaches 82.3--94.3%
best-judge top-2 recovery only for discrete intermediates in white-box,
non-adversarial tasks; its direct readout can be 35.2%, and it explicitly does
not cover diffuse intent, closed weights, or evasive computation. The barrier
can be architectural, computational, or fundamental for the chosen readout.

## Connection to Thesis Library

- **Oversight Inversion** (`the-oversight-inversion.md`): The Depth Mismatch
  offers a testable mechanism for some oversight failures. On 2607.03502's
  filler tasks, residual access plus a judge recovers more of a named
  intermediate than the filler surface verbalizes, but the paper does not
  establish zero output information or a general cost law.
- **Abstention Decay** (`the-abstention-decay.md`): RLSR (2607.03528)
  provides evidence that AURC-oriented training can improve confidence ranking.
  It does not train action-level stopping or establish why production
  abstention decays.
- **Verification Stack** (#412): DSCC (2607.03423) proposes chain-level policy
  composition. Its declared operators only tighten their represented fields;
  end-to-end security still depends on policy completeness, resource labels,
  and unrepresented-flow coverage.
- **Governance Depth** (#405): CAGE-1 Prebind Assurance (2607.03510) instantiates the thesis — prove control before consequence, not after.
- **Plausibility Gradient** (#390): Filler-token computation (2607.03502) is a
  scoped case where the surface does not verbalize the intermediate while
  task-relevant residual signal remains. “Full fidelity” is not established.
- **Repair Turn** (#419): Agentic code review (2607.03316) finds 56.3% rejection
  among comments with replies. Invalidity is the majority rejected class, while
  intent/practice misalignment is substantial but smaller; the aggregate does not
  show that all rejected suggestions “fix the wrong thing.”
- **Reasoning Precedence** (#416): aTTT (2607.03441) preserves existing reasoning competence over long horizons by detecting repetition at the token depth, not the task depth.
