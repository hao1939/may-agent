# Executable world models still need semantic validation

**Date:** 2026-11-12  
**Source:** [Evaluating LLM-Generated World Models for Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14419v1)  
**Source revision:** arXiv:2611.14419v1  
**Coverage:** Title, metadata, and abstract only  
**Disposition:** New knowledge

## Question

Is mechanical validity enough to trust a formal world model generated by an LLM for downstream planning, verification, or safety reasoning?

## What the abstract reports

The paper evaluates whether five LLMs can translate open-ended natural-language situations into formal world models represented as partially observable stochastic games. Its benchmark contains **44 scenarios** spanning game, situational, and cultural settings, and the authors assess generated models along three dimensions: mechanical validity, reference-relative structural agreement, and semantic criteria.

Across three empirical tracks, the abstract reports analysis of **410 first-pass generations**. In the reference-backed game track, **134 of 135** generated models passed a strict mechanical audit. Despite that high executable-validity rate, mean permutation-invariant structural F1 against the authored references was only **0.366**, and only **28 of the 134** mechanically valid models exactly matched the reference structure.

The cultural track reportedly showed another form of instability: explicitly naming a country changed the generated structural representation for most evaluated LLMs while causing only small changes in automated semantic scores. The authors conclude that executability is necessary but insufficient because mechanically valid formalizations can encode substantially different structures for downstream reasoning and planning.

## Scout interpretation

A generated formal artifact needs at least two separate admission gates:

1. **mechanical validity:** the artifact parses, satisfies the target schema, and can execute; and
2. **decision-problem fidelity:** its states, agents, actions, observations, transitions, rewards, and uncertainty represent the intended situation closely enough for the downstream use.

Passing the first gate proves that a model is well formed, not that it is the right model. A planner or verifier can therefore produce internally consistent results over a structurally wrong formalization. This is especially dangerous when executable output receives more trust precisely because it looks precise and machine-checkable.

For agent infrastructure, the analogous risk applies whenever an LLM converts natural-language work into a state machine, workflow, policy, dependency graph, simulator, or acceptance model. Schema validation and successful execution should be followed by checks tied to the original intent: required entities and transitions, preserved authority boundaries, invariants, counterexamples, and—when available—comparison with an independently authored reference. Where multiple formalizations are plausible, the system should expose that ambiguity rather than silently treating one executable interpretation as canonical.

The cultural-track result also warns against relying on a single scalar semantic score. If a contextual detail changes the formal structure while the score barely moves, the evaluator may be insensitive to decision-relevant differences. Validation should inspect structural deltas and their downstream consequences, not only textual or aggregate semantic similarity.
