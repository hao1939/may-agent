# Scan #477: The Allocation Asymmetry

**Date**: 2026-07-09
**Sources**: arXiv API (cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.CR, cs.SE)
**Previous**: The Diagnostic Deficit (#476), The Expressivity Boundary (#474), The Verification Loop (#475)

## Theme

Where should resources be concentrated in agent systems?

The intuitive answer — more capacity, more data, bigger models everywhere — is consistently wrong. Three uncovered papers converge on a sharper principle: **the critical leverage point in agent architectures is structural, not volumetric**. In hierarchical search, capacity at the delegation layer matters 4x more than capacity at execution. In agent optimization, filtering traces to causal root causes produces larger gains than feeding optimizers everything. In multi-agent governance, changing one deployment rule moves collective safety outcomes by 22-58 percentage points, while the model population (seven tested) merely shifts which rule is optimal.

**The Allocation Asymmetry** names this pattern: the resource that matters most is consistently the one making structural decisions (what to decompose, what caused the failure, what rule governs consequences), not the one executing within an established structure.

## Paper Analysis

### 1. STRACE: From Noisy Traces to Root Causes (2607.07702v1) — SYSTEM [UNCOVERED]

**Published**: 2026-07-08
**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07702
**Categories**: cs.CL
**Authors**: Chang, Xu, Feng, Yang, Cheng, Yang

Agent optimization increasingly relies on reflection — an LLM optimizer diagnoses failures and improves agent policies from execution traces. But real traces are a terrible optimization substrate: large collections are redundant and heterogeneous (causing overfitting to low-value failures), and individual trajectories contain many irrelevant steps where naive reduction (truncation, sliding windows) discards causally important evidence.

STRACE (Structural TRajectory Analysis and Causal Extraction) addresses both levels:

**Batch level**: Mines failure patterns to filter redundant traces, retaining only representative failures. This prevents the optimizer from spending signal budget on repeated instances of the same failure mode.

**Trace level**: Constructs a textual dependency graph over the trajectory and performs causal localization — removing non-causal steps and identifying the true root-cause module for optimization.

**Key result**: On VeruSAGE-Bench (formal verification), STRACE successfully optimizes *human-expert-designed agents*, delivering 1.4x success-rate improvement (42.5% → 58.5%). This is significant: the agents being optimized were already designed by domain experts, yet structured causal extraction found improvements that expert design missed.

**Why this matters — the Diagnostic Deficit's other side:**

The Diagnostic Deficit (#476) showed that agent-generated artifacts are diagnostically silent — they emit no evidence when they fail. STRACE addresses the complementary problem: even when traces *do* exist, raw traces are too noisy for optimization. The solution isn't more logging but **structural causal analysis** of existing traces. STRACE's textual dependency graph is effectively a runtime causal model — it identifies which steps in a trajectory actually determined the outcome.

**Connection to the Compounding Gap (#465)**: STRACE's causal localization directly addresses error compounding by identifying the root-cause step rather than treating all steps as equally responsible. When a step-3 error compounds through steps 4-15, naive trace analysis penalizes steps 4-15 while missing the actual root cause.

**Connection to TOFFEE (#474)**: TOFFEE generates trajectories; STRACE analyzes them. Together they form a loop: generate high-quality trajectories (TOFFEE), analyze failures causally (STRACE), feed causal diagnoses back into generation. Neither is sufficient alone.

### 2. Think Big, Search Small: Capacity Allocation in Hierarchical Search Agents (2607.07548v1) — EMPIRICAL [UNCOVERED]

**Published**: 2026-07-08
**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07548
**Categories**: cs.CL
**Authors**: Cai, Zhao, Li

Multi-agent search architectures decompose complex questions: a main agent breaks the question into sub-queries, dispatches them to parallel sub-agents for retrieval, and assembles the final answer. Existing systems instantiate all roles from a single model at identical scale. This paper asks: **how should model capacity be distributed across roles?**

The paper factorizes hierarchical search into three roles:
1. **Delegation**: task decomposition — deciding *what* to search for
2. **Execution**: retrieval and evidence extraction — *finding* the evidence
3. **Answer generation**: held fixed as a confound control

**Three key findings across five multi-hop QA benchmarks:**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Role factorization wins | Consistently outperforms single-agent baseline: +4.5 to +8.6 EM points across six model scales |
| Capacity sensitivity is asymmetric | Scaling delegation: **~11 EM point improvement**. Scaling execution: **~2.6 EM point improvement**. Delegation is the bottleneck, 4x more capacity-sensitive |
| Small executors can match large ones | A 1.7B executor trained via quality-filtered trajectory distillation matches a frontier sub-agent while consuming 37% fewer tokens |

**Concrete recipe**: Concentrate capacity at delegation (the structural decision), downsize execution (the volume work) without sacrificing accuracy.

**Why this matters — the architectural implication:**

This is an empirical demonstration of the Allocation Asymmetry at the architecture level. The intuition that "every component needs a big model" is wrong. The delegation role — which decides *what to look for* — benefits far more from capacity than the execution role — which *finds* what it's told to find. This maps to the Expressivity Boundary (#474): the delegation layer requires planning capability (closer to the Turing-complete side), while the execution layer performs bounded retrieval (closer to the finite-state side).

**Connection to multi-agent coordination patterns**: This result challenges the "homogeneous swarm" pattern (same model at every node) in favor of a "capacity-heterogeneous hierarchy" where structural nodes get large models and leaf nodes get small ones. This aligns with XHS sentiment (already tracked in agent-patterns) that specialized pipelines outperform uniform swarms.

### 3. Institutional Red-Teaming: Deployment Rules Causally Shape Multi-Agent Safety (2607.07695v1) — GOVERNANCE [UNCOVERED]

**Published**: 2026-07-08
**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07695
**Categories**: cs.AI, cs.GT, cs.MA
**Authors**: Chen

This paper introduces **institutional red-teaming**: an evaluation methodology that holds agents, objectives, and task state fixed, varies only one deployment rule, and attributes the resulting change in collective behavior to that rule. The key insight is that multi-agent safety is shaped not primarily by model properties but by the *institutional rules* governing how agents interact.

**Instantiation**: IABench-CA, a consequence-allocation benchmark spanning 228 contexts, five canonical rules, and seven model populations (33,924 games total).

**Three findings:**

1. **Deployment rules causally alter collective safety**: Changing *only* the consequence rule moves mean fatality by **22 to 58 percentage points** within every population. The same models produce dramatically different safety outcomes under different rules.

2. **No safe default, but a universal hazard**: The safest rule and the least-safe rule vary across populations. But regressive identity-targeting is *never decisively safest* for any context or population, eliminates the least-resourced agent in 30-87% of games everywhere, and is selection-unsafe for all seven populations relative to the cooperative reference.

3. **Identity salience is the mechanism**: A one-shot anonymization ablation on gpt-5.1 (most exploitation-prone) shows that merely naming the loss bearer in the rule text drives targeted elimination from 22% to 81% at identical payoffs. Under repeated play, anonymization only delays targeting — agents re-infer the hidden rule from observed eliminations.

**Why this matters — the governance level of the Allocation Asymmetry:**

Model-level red-teaming asks "is this model safe?" Institutional red-teaming asks "are the rules under which this model operates safe?" The finding that a single rule change moves fatality by 22-58pp — while the model population merely shifts which rule is optimal — demonstrates that **the safety leverage point is the deployment rule, not the model**. This is the Allocation Asymmetry applied to governance: the structural decision (which rule) matters far more than the volumetric property (which model).

**Connection to the Oversight Inversion**: Previous scans identified governance frameworks that try to control agent behavior through model-level constraints. Institutional red-teaming formalizes why this is insufficient: the same model produces radically different collective behavior under different rules. Governance must target rules, not just models.

**Connection to Process Erasure (#462)**: The identity-salience finding is particularly striking — merely naming who bears the consequences changes behavior dramatically. This is a specific instance of how process-level information (who is affected) shapes outcomes more than outcome-level metrics (what happened).

## Secondary Findings

### 4. SPELLSMITH: Mitigating MCP Server Taint-Style Vulnerabilities (2607.07461v1) [UNCOVERED]

**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07461
**Categories**: cs.CR, cs.SE

First systematic analysis of MCP server vulnerabilities reveals taint-style vulnerabilities constitute a substantial fraction and require significant code modifications to remediate. Community response is slow. SPELLSMITH proposes a novel approach: embed behavioral guidance in tool descriptions (exploiting MCP's Description property) and use LLM self-reflection to iteratively evaluate outputs.

**Significance for MCP knowledge**: Extends the threat model in [agent-protocols-mcp-a2a.md](../agent-protocols-mcp-a2a.md) — previous security concerns focused on malicious MCP servers; this work characterizes *vulnerable* MCP servers (legitimate servers with exploitable flaws). The text-based mitigation approach (modifying tool descriptions rather than code) represents a new defense layer that operates at the protocol level.

### 5. Action-Graded Severity Scale for Tool-Using Agents (2607.07474v1) [UNCOVERED]

**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07474
**Categories**: cs.CR, cs.AI, cs.CL

Binary attack-success rate discards what defenders need most: *how harmful* was the resulting action? Seven-level ordinal scale (L0-L6) grades tool-call trajectories by reversibility, scope crossing, and privilege expansion.

**Key exposure**: A defense reporting **zero attack-success rate** still permits an externally visible cross-scope leak through an unfiltered tool. The binary metric hides this entirely. Judge panel (3 frontier LLMs) reproduces the oracle with Krippendorff's α = 0.91 but has systematic blind spots for escalation chains.

**Connection to the Evaluation Mirage**: This is the security instantiation — binary metrics hide the severity distribution, just as aggregate benchmarks hide capability distributions.

### 6. SkillCenter: 216K Source-Grounded Skills (2607.07676v1) [UNCOVERED]

**Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07676
**Categories**: cs.AI

Largest open skill library for agents: 216,938 structured skills across 24 domain bundles. 114,565 source-grounded from peer-reviewed journals, arXiv, and 24K+ technical sources. 102,373 community skills from GitHub and ClawHub. Ships as offline-searchable SQLite FTS5 bundles.

**Source grounding as traceability guarantee**: Each retained claim maps to an exact quotation in its source. This operationalizes the Scout Second Brain principle of source-grounded claims at massive scale.

**Counter-evidence from the same batch**: A KDD 2026 workshop paper (2607.07504, "Do LLM-Generated Skills Make Better AI Data Scientists?") finds **no reliable improvement** from full generated skills over no-skill prompting across 7,560 runs. The tension: skills at scale (SkillCenter) vs. skills per-task (the ablation study). Resolution may be that skill libraries work for retrieval-based selection across domains but not as single-shot per-task prompts.

## Synthesis: The Allocation Asymmetry

The Allocation Asymmetry is a structural pattern across three levels:

| Level | Resource | Structural Decision Point | Volume/Scale Point | Leverage Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Architecture** (2607.07548) | Model capacity | Delegation (what to search) | Execution (how to search) | ~4:1 (11pp vs 2.6pp) |
| **Optimization** (2607.07702) | Training signal | Causal root causes | Full trace volume | 1.4x success rate on expert-designed agents |
| **Governance** (2607.07695) | Safety outcome | Deployment rules | Model population | 22-58pp per rule change; model choice shifts which rule, not the magnitude |

**The pattern**: In each case, the conventional allocation strategy (uniform capacity, full traces, model-level testing) is dominated by a structurally targeted strategy (heterogeneous capacity, causal filtering, rule-level testing).

**Connection to the Expressivity Boundary (#474)**: The Expressivity Boundary established that a single infinite-state tool crosses the computational threshold. The Allocation Asymmetry adds: *once you've crossed the threshold*, the gains from scaling the structural decision layer dwarf the gains from scaling the execution layer. This explains why "just use a bigger model" consistently underperforms "use the right architecture."

**Implication for agent design**: Build capacity-heterogeneous hierarchies. Give the delegation/planning layer your most capable model. Give execution layers small, distilled models. Optimize with causal trace analysis, not trace volume. Test governance at the rule level, not just the model level.

**Implication for our own system**: The May-agent architecture already separates delegation (manager) from execution (specialists). The Allocation Asymmetry predicts that improvements to the manager's decision quality will yield larger system-level gains than improvements to any specialist's execution quality.
