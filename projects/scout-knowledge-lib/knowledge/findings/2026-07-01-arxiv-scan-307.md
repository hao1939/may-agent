# arXiv Scan #307: The Equivalence Mask

**Date**: 2026-07-01 | **Scan**: #307 | **Source**: arXiv (cs.AI, cs.MA, cs.CL)

## Theme

Six independent papers converge on a structural finding that extends the library's Evaluation Mirage and Observability Trap: **outcome-level measurement conceals divergent internal states.** Systems that appear equivalent by surface metrics are structurally different underneath — in control cost, knowledge organization, belief-to-prediction transfer, aggregation robustness, answer composition, and user-knowledge expansion.

This is not the claim that "metrics are imperfect." It is the stronger claim that outcome equivalence is a *mask* — the same surface result actively hides structural differences that determine whether a system degrades, scales, or fails under novel conditions.

## The Six Convergent Papers

### 1. HASTE: Hierarchical Skill Accumulation (2606.30911)

**Finding**: Holding a 159-skill inventory constant across 8 ML-engineering competitions, *tiered loading* (global → domain → competition-specific) achieves **100% medal rate** while *flat loading* achieves **62.5%** — the same rate as loading no skills at all. Same knowledge, different organization → divergent outcomes.

**Structural implication**: Knowledge organization is not an optimization — it is a structural determinant. Flat loading is *equivalent to no loading* by outcome, but the two states are entirely different in what they represent. Warm starts use 52% fewer refinement iterations and acceptance rate rises from 42% to 85% once 50+ scoped skills accumulate. The mask: "agent has 159 skills" conceals whether those skills are *structurally accessible*.

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30911
- Venue: ICML 2026 Workshop on Deep Learning for Code (DL4C)

### 2. Regulatory Hysteresis (2606.30975)

**Finding**: The adaptive gain required to regulate an artificial agent forms a reproducible **hysteresis loop** — the same uncertainty target requires different levels of control depending on whether the agent is moving toward or returning from a more demanding regime. Anticipatory regulation reaches comparable regulated behavior with lower control demand.

**Structural implication**: Two agents at the same measured state may require radically different regulatory effort. An agent returning from a stress regime looks the same as one approaching it, but the returning agent needs more control to maintain that appearance. The mask: "agent is at target state X" conceals the *control burden* required to stay there.

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30975

### 3. BayesBench: Belief–Prediction Gap (2606.30850)

**Finding**: Across seven LLMs (3B–70B), scaling improves latent inference — belief updates occasionally matching the Bayesian posterior — but **these gains do not reliably transfer to downstream prediction**. The gap between inferring latent structure and using it to predict outcomes persists at all scales.

**Structural implication**: An agent that appears to "understand" (good latent inference) may still fail to act on that understanding (poor prediction). The mask: "model correctly infers the hidden variable" conceals whether that inference connects to action. This is a formal demonstration of a gap the library has tracked informally — comprehension ≠ competence — now with Bayesian baselines that pin the specific failure mode (inference-to-prediction transfer, not inference itself).

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30850

### 4. RoPoLL: Robust Panel of LLM Judges (2606.30931)

**Finding**: The standard Panel of LLM Evaluators (PoLL) incurs **unbounded bias** under any positive contamination, regardless of jury size, whenever a single judge fails in a biased way (mode collapse, sycophancy, safety refusal). Replacing the aggregation function with geometric median (RoPoLL) restores bounded error. A 3-judge RoPoLL committee at 38B beats Mistral-Large-3 (675B) by 1.31× under 30% corruption — an 18× parameter advantage.

**Structural implication**: Aggregation method determines whether multi-agent evaluation is robust or catastrophically fragile. The mask: "we have N independent judges" conceals whether the aggregation function propagates or contains failure. Mean-based consensus is *more dangerous* than a single judge because it provides false confidence. This directly extends the library's Evaluation Mirage: the mirage isn't just quantized scores, it's that the *aggregation architecture* determines whether the panel carries signal.

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30931

### 5. Multi-Agent Deliberation in Law (2606.30906)

**Finding**: Multi-agent frameworks inspired by courtroom procedures achieve **comparable overall performance** to baseline LLMs on legal reasoning tasks — but produce **significantly distinct answers**. They solve cases the baseline cannot (and vice versa). Multi-agent approaches are especially stronger on questions requiring critical thinking from multiple perspectives.

**Structural implication**: Comparable accuracy masks complementary coverage. Two systems at the same aggregate score may have entirely different failure modes. The mask: "system achieves X% accuracy" conceals the *composition of that accuracy* — which items are solved and which are missed. This matters for governance: a system that fails on "easy" cases but solves "hard" ones is structurally different from one that does the reverse, even if their overall scores match.

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30906
- Venue: AIDA2J Workshop, 21st International Conference of AI & Law, Singapore

### 6. CoPref/CoShop: Preference Construction (2606.30863)

**Finding**: Five frontier models cannot exceed **56% accuracy** on CoShop, not because they fail to find items, but because **interactions don't expand what users know about what they want**. The paper introduces the Search-Experience-Credence framework: users often lack domain knowledge to have fully specified preferences, and agents that only *elicit* preferences (instead of helping *construct* them) fail systematically.

**Structural implication**: An agent that appears responsive and helpful may be completely failing at the actual task — expanding user understanding. The mask: "agent conducted N dialogue turns and made recommendations" conceals whether the interaction actually changed the user's knowledge state. This extends the library's Comprehension Collapse theme (scan #303): the comprehension gap isn't just in agent outputs — it's in the interaction itself.

- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30863

## The Equivalence Mask Pattern

All six papers share a common structure:

| Paper | Surface Metric | Hidden State | Governance Consequence |
|-------|---------------|--------------|----------------------|
| HASTE | # of skills loaded | Scope-tier organization | Flat = useless despite appearing "equipped" |
| Hysteresis | Current regulated state | Control burden / history | Same state, different fragility |
| BayesBench | Latent inference accuracy | Inference-to-prediction transfer | Good understanding ≠ good action |
| RoPoLL | Panel size / model count | Aggregation function | More judges can mean more bias |
| Legal MAD | Overall accuracy | Item-level coverage pattern | Same score, different failure modes |
| CoPref | Dialogue turn count | User knowledge expansion | More interaction ≠ better alignment |

**The design principle**: Surface metrics are not degraded signals — they are masks that actively conceal the structural dimension that determines system behavior under stress, scale, or novelty. Governance based exclusively on outcome metrics is not imprecise; it is *structurally blind* to the dimensions that determine whether a system will fail.
