## Evidence: Three Uncovered Papers

### 1. Temperature Is Not Determinism (2606.26185)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26185

**The problem:** LLM-as-judge safety evaluations assume temperature=0 produces deterministic results. The authors test this against Japan AISI's open-source evaluation harness (aisev).

**Key findings:**
- The harness invokes its grader **without setting temperature or seed** — the provider silently applies its default of 1.0
- Per-item disagreement reaches **~50% over 20 identical runs** on borderline items
- Pinning temperature=0 reduces but does not eliminate flips: across 690 API calls, 2 providers, 3 model tiers, and 5 sampling configurations, **1-2 of 7 borderline items remain non-reproducible** even under forced greedy decoding (top_k=1)
- Claude Opus 4.7/4.8 has **deprecated temperature entirely**, rendering the primary mitigation inapplicable to newer models

**Structural implication:** Single-run safety verdicts present noise as a safety property. Evaluation harnesses that gate deployment decisions on one grader pass are making decisions that would change on re-run — and they don't know it.

**Connection to library:** This is the Evaluation Mirage applied to safety evaluation specifically. The Mirage showed 3 bits of Shannon entropy across 14,345 evaluations; this paper shows that even those 3 bits aren't reproducible. The 10 quantized scores our system produces (Evaluation Mirage §Layer 1) are more stable than production safety evaluations — because our heuristic evaluator, paradoxically, doesn't use stochastic sampling at all. Our weakness (crude buckets) is their strength (deterministic).

### 2. Poisoned Playbooks: Knowledge as Attack Vector (2606.24402)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24402

**The problem:** Security agents use RAG to retrieve vulnerability write-ups. What happens when the knowledge base is poisoned?

**Key findings:**
- A **single poisoned write-up** injected into public-style security knowledge alters agent behavior systematically
- Across 11 CTF challenges, 3 frontier LLM families, 2 model generations, 11 real-world CVEs: **poison adoption is systematic, not random**
- The authors introduce the **Verification Boundary (VB)** — a 3-level classification based on what evidence the agent can use to refute a retrieved claim:
  - **VB-1 (Strong):** Agent can verify against direct observable evidence → poison often rejected
  - **VB-2 (Moderate):** Agent can partially cross-reference → poison sometimes adopted
  - **VB-3 (Weak/Zero-day):** No independent evidence available → poison **systematically adopted**
- Verification prompting and multi-source retrieval help at VB-1/VB-2 but **weaken under VB-3 (sparse/zero-day) conditions**

**Structural implication:** The Verification Boundary concept is the first empirical classification of *when* agents can resist poisoned inputs vs. when resistance is structurally impossible. At VB-3, agents have no independent evidence and must either accept the claim or refuse to act. This is the Verification Inversion applied to the knowledge layer: input quality determines output quality, but input verification is structurally bounded.

**Connection to library:** Extends Memory Authority (2606.24322, scan #191), the Verification Commons Collapse (#144), and the Unfireable Safety Kernel (2606.26057). The VB framework maps directly onto the Memory Authority paper's distinction between content integrity and lineage integrity — at VB-3, neither form of evidence is available.

### 3. Intent-Aware Safety: The Missing Signal (2606.27210)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27210

**The problem:** Safety classifiers operate on prompts and labels, but never model what the user is *trying to do*.

**Key findings:**
- AIMS dataset: 1,724 difficult safety prompts, each with human-annotated **intent descriptions** and harm labels
- Intent-conditioned DPO outperforms SFT; intent-conditioned distillation outperforms reasoning-only distillation in most teacher-student pairs
- **GRPO (directly rewarding intent faithfulness)** yields strongest average performance across 5 external safety benchmarks
- Intent-aware models form the **inference latency–F1 Pareto frontier** — faster AND more accurate than intent-agnostic alternatives

**Structural implication:** Safety classification has been operating without its most informative signal. Intent is a compact, high-quality supervision signal that subsumes the noisy heuristics classifiers currently use. This explains why prompt-level safety gates produce both false positives (blocking benign intents) and false negatives (missing harmful intents dressed in polite language).

**Connection to library:** This is the CalVerT principle (2606.21777, scan #207) generalized to safety: agent-external epistemic signals outperform agent-internal heuristics. CalVerT supplies calibrated confidence; AIMS supplies intent. Both are *the signal the model can't infer reliably from the prompt alone*. Also connects to the Anti-Deliberation Principle (#176): external signals beat internal reasoning.

### The Response Bifurcation

The field's response to anchor erosion is splitting into two incompatible strategies:

**Behavioral Anchors** (patch the existing surface):
- Verification prompting (#2, mitigates at VB-1/2, fails at VB-3)
- Intent modeling (#3, requires human-annotated intent — finite supply)
- Multi-run evaluation (reproduce → disagree → flag)
- Social engineering detection (style-mismatch, timezone, domain checks)

**Architectural Anchors** (move trust outside the eroding layer):
- Process separation (Unfireable Safety Kernel, 2606.26057 — trust in kernel, not agent)
- Sandboxing (MicroVMs 348pts, Tupper, NexusBox — trust in isolation)
- Cryptographic memory (2606.24322 — trust in math, not content)
- Intent as external state (CalVerT, AIMS — trust the signal, not the inference)

The **behavioral** side is losing. Every behavioral defense is contingent on the layer beneath it being trustworthy — and that layer is also eroding. Verification prompting fails at VB-3 because there's no evidence to verify against. Intent modeling requires human annotation because models can't reliably infer intent. Multi-run evaluation requires reproducible sampling, which Claude has deprecated.

The **architectural** side has a structural advantage: it doesn't trust the eroding layers at all. Process separation doesn't ask "is this safe?" — it enforces "this path is blocked." Sandboxing doesn't ask "is this code malicious?" — it limits blast radius. Cryptographic memory doesn't ask "is this content trustworthy?" — it proves provenance.

### The Key Insight

Behavioral trust is a promissory note backed by other promissory notes. Architectural trust is a constraint enforced by physics (process isolation), mathematics (cryptographic proofs), or mechanism design (fail-closed defaults). The entire governance discourse — 1086pts of it — is negotiating behavioral trust. The infrastructure that will actually determine agent safety is being built silently, beneath the discourse, by practitioners who stopped waiting.
