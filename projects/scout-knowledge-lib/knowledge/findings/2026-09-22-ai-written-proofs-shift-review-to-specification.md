# AI-written proofs shift the review bottleneck to specification

**Date observed:** 2026-09-22  
**Source:** Hacker News item [49796630](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49796630)  
**Primary source:** [Lean Verified Transformers](https://srush.github.io/lean-transformer/)  
**Repo:** [srush/lean-transformer](https://github.com/srush/lean-transformer)  
**Coverage:** Full rendered article reviewed; repository linked but not audited or built.  
**Disposition:** New knowledge.

## Finding

Sasha Rush's demonstration gives a concrete form to an emerging human/AI division of labor in formal verification. The author says the exposition, specifications, and structure are human-written while all proofs are AI-written. The page presents Lean definitions and completed proof states for elementary neural-network and Transformer properties, including selection equivariance, per-example batch invariance, tensor- and data-parallel decomposition, locality of sliding-window attention, tiled attention equivalence, and tiled state-space scans.

The important agent-design lesson is not simply that an AI can produce proof scripts. It is that a proof-producing agent can return an independently checkable artifact rather than asking a reviewer to trust a generated explanation or a finite collection of tests. This can make formal obligations practical for optimization boundaries where ordinary tests sample only some shapes and inputs. A useful workflow is:

1. a human or trusted specification process states the invariant and abstraction boundary;
2. an agent proposes the proof;
3. a small trusted checker accepts or rejects the proof artifact;
4. the build ties that checked theorem to the exact implementation revision and configuration it is meant to cover.

That last two-part boundary matters. The Lean kernel checks that the theorem follows from the encoded definitions; it does not establish that those definitions faithfully describe production kernels. In this demonstration, vectors and tensors are functions from finite indices to rational numbers, explicitly chosen to avoid storage and efficiency concerns. The conclusion names quantization error, aggregation, backpropagation, and train/inference mismatch as work still to consider. Consequently, a theorem about exact rational arithmetic cannot by itself certify floating-point kernels, distributed reduction order, memory layout, or generated accelerator code.

## Implication for agentic engineering

Proof-generating agents need a **specification review gate**, not only a proof-checking gate. Reviews should record:

- the exact theorem and assumptions;
- the concrete code, model, or IR linked to those definitions;
- excluded semantics such as floating-point rounding, nondeterminism, storage aliases, or cross-host communication;
- the checker/toolchain version and a reproducible build receipt;
- regression obligations that fail when the implementation-to-model link drifts.

This suggests a stronger evidence hierarchy for coding agents: a successful test demonstrates sampled behavior; a checked proof demonstrates a universal property of a formal model; neither demonstrates that the formal model is the deployed implementation unless the refinement link is also established. As proof generation gets cheaper, specification coverage and refinement evidence become the scarce review work.

## Limits

This scan did not clone or build the repository, replay the proofs, inspect commit history, or independently establish that every proof was AI-generated. The author reports that provenance. The article is a pedagogical model using exact rational arithmetic rather than a verification of a production Transformer stack, and it does not measure proof-generation cost, failure rate, reviewer effort, or maintenance under implementation changes. The HN item had no comments when checked, so it adds discovery provenance rather than independent technical review.
