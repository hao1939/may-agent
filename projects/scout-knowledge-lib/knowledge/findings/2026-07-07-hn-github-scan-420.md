## Structural Analysis: The Proof Requirement

### What Changed

The Repair Turn (#419) showed the bottleneck shifted from detection to repair. This scan shows the community's **engagement-level response**: demand proof before extending trust.

Six proof types visible on one front page:
1. **Formal proof** (Kani — mathematical correctness)
2. **Behavioral proof** (Fable 5 — agent character verification)
3. **Semantic proof** (token pricing — metric validity)
4. **Mechanistic proof** (Anthropic GW — model internal transparency)
5. **Regulatory proof** (Nintendo — mandated physical verifiability)
6. **Infrastructure proof** (rail map — system state visibility)

### The Ratio Signal

Kani's ratio (21.6) deserves special attention. In tracking history:
- High-controversy stories: ratio <1.0 (DayQuil 0.75, Emily Bender 0.80-0.94)
- Moderate consensus: ratio 1.5-3.0 (most sovereignty stories)
- Strong consensus: ratio 3.0-7.0 (education, infrastructure)
- **Near-universal agreement**: ratio >10.0 (Kani 21.6)

A ratio >20 means the community doesn't need to discuss the topic — the consensus is pre-existing. Formal verification for Rust isn't controversial; it's **overdue**. The community votes with silent upvotes rather than debate.

### Design Principle

**Proof precedes trust.** The community's trust architecture now requires evidence at the same layer as the claim:
- Capability claim → behavioral proof (Fable 5)
- Cost claim → semantic proof (token pricing)
- Safety claim → mechanistic proof (interpretability)
- Ownership claim → regulatory proof (EU mandates)
- Correctness claim → formal proof (Kani)

Systems that provide proof at the wrong layer — or no proof at all — lose trust regardless of actual quality. The proof requirement is not about skepticism of AI per se; it's about demanding that claims be falsifiable and verified at the appropriate level of abstraction.
